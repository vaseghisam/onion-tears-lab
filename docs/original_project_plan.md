# Why Onions Make Us Cry — and Which Tricks Actually Help

## Project brief

**Series:** The Serious Science of Annoyances  
**Document:** Article and research project plan, version 1  
**Date:** 21 September 2026  
**Status:** Proposed investigation. No onion simulations or experiments have yet been performed.

The article will follow the irritation from the knife's first cut to the eye, then use that causal sequence to assess practical remedies. Its scientific subjects are tissue damage, enzyme chemistry, release into air, transport, and sensory response. Its everyday question is which changes to preparation reduce irritation, under which conditions, and with what evidence.

I recommend proceeding with this topic. It sustains the series' attention to a familiar nuisance while introducing a different scientific problem: a chemical source whose production and transport can respond differently to the same intervention. The principal computational contribution should be a comparison of exposure under specified cutting and airflow conditions. The chemical and physiological account should rest on published evidence.

The provisional title uses **"help"** because a reduction in exposure does not establish complete prevention. Retain the original **"actually prevent it"** only if the completed evidence review supports that promise under clearly stated conditions.

The intended finished article is approximately 3,000–4,000 words, excluding references and a separate technical supplement. This is a working length, not a quota. The main text should remain continuous, accessible prose, with equations introduced where they resolve a question the reader already understands.

## Continuity with the earlier articles

This plan draws on the available egg manuscript, *The Physics of a Cleanly Peeled Egg*, and the saved Markdown version of *Why a Shower Curtain Attacks You*. These were read as working precedents; their identity with the final published versions has not been checked.

The egg article moves from a practical observation through transport and material response to a conditional account of separation. The shower-curtain article develops force balances, compares mechanisms through calculations, and explains the distinction between numerical verification and experimental validation. The onion article should preserve that progression and the same care with what a model establishes.

The computational scale need not match the shower project. Begin with an explicit release model and a controlled transport calculation. Add tissue fracture, droplets, or full kitchen airflow only when the evidence shows that the added detail changes an article-level conclusion.

## 1. The central question

**How do cutting, temperature, airflow, and protection change the amount and timing of onion irritant that reaches the eyes?**

This question connects three different quantities that must remain distinct throughout the article:

| Quantity | What it describes | What would establish it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Chemical production and release | Irritant formed in damaged tissue and emitted into air | Chemical assays and time-resolved headspace measurements |
| Exposure near the eye | Airborne concentration, its duration, and potentially droplet deposition | A transport calculation or measurements at a stated position |
| Irritation and tearing | The person's sensory and reflex response | Appropriate human observations or published physiological experiments |

Lower droplet counts, lower total volatile emissions, lower concentration at eye height, and fewer tears are different findings. A remedy must be evaluated against the outcome actually measured.

The article's proposed organizing idea is that remedies intervene at different stages. Some may alter the source, some the route through the air, and some the entry of irritant around the eyes. Whether combining them helps, and by how much, remains a question for the evidence and calculations.

## 2. Establish the chemistry before choosing model parameters

One primary result already provides a sound starting point. Imai and colleagues identified lachrymatory-factor synthase as the enzyme that specifically produces the onion lachrymatory factor, correcting the earlier account of its spontaneous formation after alliinase action. The publisher's abstract and bibliographic record were checked for this plan.[1]

The full research phase will verify and document the sequence from the onion precursor isoalliin, through alliinase-mediated production of a sulfenic-acid intermediate, to syn-propanethial S-oxide through lachrymatory-factor synthase. Exact molecular names, stereochemistry, cellular localization, and competing reactions must be checked against the primary chemistry before drawing the reaction figure.

Explain an enzyme when it first appears as a molecule that catalyses a chemical reaction. Define "lachrymatory" simply as tear-inducing, then use the shorter term "irritant" when chemical specificity is unnecessary.

Several common explanations require special scrutiny:

- Do not repeat the claim that onion fumes turn into sulfuric acid in the eyes without direct chemical evidence. Identify the actual supported irritant and physiological pathway.
- Do not substitute garlic chemistry, allicin, or findings on other sulfur compounds for onion lachrymatory-factor chemistry.
- Do not name a particular sensory receptor as established for this molecule solely because it responds to other food irritants.
- Distinguish biological interpretation of the pathway as plant defence from experimentally demonstrated functions.
- Treat flavour, perceived sweetness, sulfur chemistry, and lachrymatory activity as related but distinct properties.

Gene-silencing experiments offer a potentially useful causal comparison. Eady and colleagues' study of lachrymatory-factor synthase suppression should be retrieved to examine both the reduction in lachrymatory factor and changes elsewhere in sulfur metabolism.[2] Joyce and colleagues' analytical work is a candidate source on chemical characterization of these onions.[3] Neither study's complete text was accessible during preparation of this plan; detailed findings remain to be checked.

## 3. Evidence review and remedy assessment

Build a claim ledger before drafting. For each claim, record the source, exact supporting passage or figure, onion type, treatment, comparator, measured outcome, sample size, uncertainty, and limits of transfer to domestic cutting. Record commercial affiliations where relevant to cultivar or product comparisons.

Search primary literature through the execution date. Keep the search terms, databases, inclusion decisions, and inaccessible sources in a short research log. Secondary explanations can supply leads; numerical claims and mechanistic conclusions should return to the original study.

Give each finding one of four labels: **direct intervention evidence**, **mechanistic evidence**, **model-dependent prediction**, or **unresolved**. A source can support more than one label, but the evidence for a practical remedy must be stated separately from the evidence for its proposed mechanism.

The following table is the investigation agenda, not a completed ranking of remedies.

| Intervention | Mechanism to investigate | Evidence needed and main qualification |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Well-fitting protective goggles | Restriction of irritant entry around the eyes | Examine onion-specific evidence and fit or leakage; ordinary spectacles are not an equivalent enclosure |
| Airflow directed away from the face | Change in the route from source to eye | Direction, speed, geometry, and eye-position concentration; a fan can redistribute emissions as well as remove them locally |
| Local extraction | Capture before irritant spreads through the room | Capture efficiency at the cutting board; hood airflow rating alone is insufficient |
| Chilled onion | Changes to reaction rates, volatility, and tissue mechanics | Actual tissue temperature, release history, and rewarming; do not assume every effect has the same sign |
| Sharp knife | Change in damaged tissue and liquid ejection | Controlled blade geometry and sharpness, equal cutting task, chemical or exposure measurements |
| Slower individual cuts | Change in mechanical ejection | Compare against the longer time needed to complete the same task; lower instantaneous release may coexist with longer exposure |
| Brief rinsing, soaking, or cutting under water | Removal, dissolution, or interception of compounds | Keep these treatments distinct; quantify duration and any change to flavour, texture, handling, and waste water |
| Leaving the root end intact | Different tissue distribution or less damage | Spatial chemical measurements and matched cut area; do not assume the root contains all the relevant compounds |
| Mild or low-lachrymatory cultivars | Different precursor or enzyme activity | Cultivar-specific assays; do not equate sweetness or low pungency with zero lachrymatory activity |
| Wet towel or bowl of water beside the board | Possible uptake at a nearby wet surface | Competing transport paths and capture rate; the presence of water does not establish interception before the eyes |
| Bread in the mouth, chewing gum, spoon, or candle | Claimed interception or change in breathing or airflow | Seek controlled evidence; avoid supplying an invented mechanism when evidence is absent |
| Oil coating | Possible modification of liquid breakup or release | Assess the original experimental conditions, chemical exposure endpoint, and handling before considering practical advice |

Chilling and freezing must be evaluated separately. Breathing through the mouth should not be treated as an established way to prevent an eye-surface exposure. Contact lenses should not be proposed as protective equipment.

At completion, the reader's table should contain only four columns: method, supported benefit, evidence strength, and practical limitation. Use numerical efficacy only when a source or validated calculation supports that particular number. "No controlled evidence located" is different from "shown not to work."

## 4. A specific research opportunity: vapour and droplets

Reports encountered during the initial search describe research on liquid ejection during onion cutting and the effects of blade sharpness and speed. The primary study and its publication status were not verified in this preparation, so no experimental numbers or conclusions from those reports are adopted here.

Locating that study is a priority because it may connect this article to a substantive mechanics problem. Check what was actually measured: droplets, their size and velocity, chemical composition, airborne lachrymatory factor, or human tears. Determine whether results from individual onion layers extend to a whole bulb and an ordinary cutting task.

The article must distinguish molecular vapour from suspended liquid droplets. A visible mist is not a measurement of all volatile irritant, and a droplet count does not establish which route dominates exposure at the eyes.

If useful measurements exist, compare a vapour-only transport model with a model that includes a documented droplet contribution. If the chemical partition between those routes is unknown, vary it as an explicit sensitivity assumption. Do not describe a simulation as having discovered the dominant route when that dominance was specified as an input.

## 5. Computational programme

### Model A. Production and release after a cut

First ask whether published time-resolved release measurements are available. If they are, use an empirical emission history directly for the transport model. Reconstructing enzyme kinetics is unnecessary unless it helps explain a remedy or a measured time dependence.

If data are insufficient, use a reduced model to expose assumptions. Let $P(t)$ be the accessible precursor amount and $L(t)$ the retained irritant amount, both in moles. One possible approximation is

$$\frac{dP}{dt}=I_P(t)-k_pP.$$

$$\frac{dL}{dt}=y k_pP-(k_e+k_d)L.$$

$$Q(t)=k_eL.$$

Here $I_P$ is precursor made accessible by cutting in moles per second; $k_p$, $k_e$, and $k_d$ are effective conversion, emission, and loss rates in inverse seconds; $y$ is the molar yield assigned to the lumped pathway; and $Q$ is the emission rate in moles per second. A single cut can instead be represented by initial accessible precursor with $I_P=0$ thereafter.

This compresses several reactions and transfer processes. It is not a measured enzyme mechanism or a calibrated onion model. Add saturation kinetics or an explicit intermediate only if source data make those parameters identifiable and the additional state changes a result used in the article.

Use temperature dependence only where documented. An Arrhenius approximation may be considered over an appropriate range, with a sourced activation energy; do not invent one to produce a desired chilling benefit. When the data are missing, compare specified release histories and label them as hypothetical scenarios.

**Outputs:** emission versus time; total emitted amount; sensitivity to release and loss times; comparisons of a single cut and a fixed repeated-cut task.

### Model B. Transport from board to eye position

The principal calculation will transport a dilute irritant through prescribed air motion:

$$\frac{\partial c}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot(\mathbf u c)=\nabla\cdot(D_{\mathrm{eff}}\nabla c)-\lambda c+s(\mathbf x,t).$$

Here $c$ is gas-phase concentration in moles per cubic metre, $\mathbf u$ is air velocity in metres per second, $D_{\mathrm{eff}}$ is molecular diffusivity or an explicitly stated effective mixing coefficient in square metres per second, $\lambda$ is an assumed first-order loss rate, and $s$ is source strength per unit volume. For a volume source, require $\int s\,dV=Q(t)$; a surface-flux implementation must satisfy the equivalent surface integral.

Begin with an analytic three-dimensional puff benchmark and a simple numerical domain. Use a finite source region and a small observation volume near the eye position to avoid point-source singularities. A two-dimensional display must be identified as a slice of a three-dimensional solution, or explicitly as a two-dimensional approximation with different dilution.

Compare still air in the idealized model, airflow away from the observer, airflow toward the observer, and transverse airflow. Keep source amount, geometry, scales, and observation duration fixed for the initial comparison. Distinguish this mechanism-isolation exercise from later comparisons of complete preparation tasks.

State initial conditions, clean inflow, outflow treatment, impermeable surfaces, and any assumed surface uptake. Ideal still air excludes human thermal plumes and room drafts. Add those only if the core result depends on them. A prescribed velocity field is not a computed kitchen flow.

The characteristic competition between air motion and molecular diffusion can be expressed through

$$\mathrm{Pe}=\frac{U\ell}{D}.$$

Here $U$ is characteristic air speed, $\ell$ the board-to-observation distance, and $D$ molecular diffusivity. If an effective coefficient replaces $D$, state that this is a different, coarse-grained comparison. Use this relation to explain the relevant transport regime rather than introduce an arbitrary new index of annoyance.

**Outputs:** concentration maps, concentration histories near the eyes, peak concentration, and concentration integrated over time. All absolute values require a supported source strength; otherwise normalize by a stated common reference and report conditional ratios.

### Model C. Exposure and protection

Let $c_e(t)$ be concentration averaged over the observation volume. Define an exposure proxy

$$E(t_f)=\int_0^{t_f}c_e(t)\,dt.$$

Its units are mole-seconds per cubic metre. It is not an absorbed ocular dose, a tear volume, or a validated irritation score. A shorter intense exposure and a longer weak exposure can have the same integral, so show peak concentration and the full time history as well.

A first comparison of a protective enclosure may use

$$c_{\mathrm{inside}}(t)=\beta c_e(t),\qquad 0\leq\beta\leq1.$$

Here $\beta$ is an assumed penetration factor. This is a sensitivity model, not a measured rating for goggles. Use a small-volume exchange model instead if leakage and accumulation data justify it. Do not assign a universal tearing threshold or fit a physiological response curve without relevant measurements.

**Outputs:** exposure ratios for specified changes to source, transport, and penetration; explicit statements of which factors are assumed. Multiplication of independent reduction factors is permissible only within the fixed linear model. Test interactions when a treatment also changes source release or airflow.

### Optional extension. Droplet mechanics

Proceed only if the original cutting study supplies usable size, speed, and release data. Model drag and gravitational settling over the documented range, and include evaporation or gas-phase exchange only with an explicit closure and mass accounting. Molecular vapour cannot be represented as ballistic droplets.

A full fracture calculation of onion tissue or a coupled room-scale computational-fluid-dynamics model is outside the initial scope. Either becomes useful only if the simpler calculations cannot answer an essential question and suitable material or boundary data exist.

## 6. Comparisons that would earn a place in the article

The numerical work should resolve a few questions well:

1. **How much does airflow direction matter at a fixed observation position?** Compare otherwise identical sources and flow magnitudes, then vary position to show the limits of any advice about fan placement.
2. **Does a lower peak imply less exposure over the whole preparation task?** Compare matched onion amounts and final cut sizes, include any extra preparation time, and continue through a stated post-cut interval. Keep this distinct from a fixed-duration source comparison.
3. **How does uncertainty in release affect the practical conclusions?** Test whether qualitative rankings persist across alternative release histories, distances, and mixing assumptions.
4. **Can transport changes alter release itself?** Compare the prescribed-source assumption with a simple mass-transfer-limited source if evidence warrants it. Moving air may affect both removal from tissue and delivery to the observer.

Chilling comparisons should include rewarming and a sufficiently long observation window to distinguish delayed release from a reduction in total release. A conclusion about the best complete preparation method requires that wider comparison.

Do not select parameter values to make a preferred trick win. If rankings reverse, show the conditions responsible. If available evidence cannot constrain a comparison, omit its numerical ranking and retain the mechanistic explanation.

## 9. Empirical work, if it would resolve an important gap

The article can be completed from published research and transparent conditional calculations. A new household trial is optional and must not be presented as necessary validation of every model.

For useful chemical measurements, prioritize a published dataset or an appropriately equipped collaborator. A generic volatile-organic-compound sensor, odour rating, or video of mist does not specifically quantify onion lachrymatory factor. Sampling delay, recovery, chemical stability, and discrimination from other volatiles must be established before interpreting an assay.

If a small practical comparison is later undertaken, specify onion batch and storage, tissue temperature, mass, final cut size, knife, timing, eye distance, room air, and the outcome in advance. Use multiple bulbs, randomize order, and account for the fact that repeated pieces from one bulb are not independent bulbs. Matched halves can reduce variability but cannot by themselves eliminate position, order, or carryover effects. Determine replication from pilot variation and the desired precision rather than inventing a sample size that appears authoritative.

Subjective reports can support a clearly labelled exploratory observation. A controlled claim about tear prevention would need a suitable human-study design and the applicable consent and review arrangements. The core publication does not depend on deliberately provoking eye irritation.

## 10. Verification, uncertainty, and publication conditions

The technical work should include dimensional checks, nonnegative concentrations and inventories, integrated mass accounting, and a benchmark against analytic diffusion and constant-velocity transport. Check zero source, zero flow, zero assumed loss, and the limiting penetration factors. Track chemical conversion losses separately from emission rather than treating all disappearing precursor as airborne irritant.

Refine mesh, time step, and domain size for quantities actually reported in the article. Choose an initial numerical acceptance target, such as less than 5% change in reported exposure ratios across successive refinements, and disclose any unresolved cases. Such a target screens numerical sensitivity; it is not a guarantee of accuracy in a real kitchen.

Separate three questions in the uncertainty account: whether the solver implements its equations, whether plausible parameter choices change the result, and whether measurements support the physical model. Scenario envelopes are not statistical confidence intervals. Report a confidence interval only when a defined sampling or inference procedure warrants it.

Before publication, conduct a dedicated claim-and-mathematics audit followed by an editorial continuity pass. An independent human or agent review can be added when available and explicitly authorized. No claim of independent review should appear until it has occurred.

Release the article only when each practical recommendation has an identified evidential basis, every plotted result can be regenerated, every figure says what kind of evidence it contains, and the final title matches the conclusions.

That result determines how much numerical detail the article can responsibly carry.
